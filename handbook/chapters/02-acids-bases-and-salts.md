## Chapter 2: Acids, Bases and Salts

### Learning goals

Study the key concepts, explanations, examples, and evidence in this chapter.

Acids in aqueous solution produce hydrogen ions; bases in aqueous solution produce hydroxide ions in the introductory model. An indicator changes colour over a range of acidity. Litmus distinguishes acidic and basic conditions, while universal indicator gives an approximate pH colour. A lower pH indicates a more acidic solution and a higher pH a more basic solution; pH is not a direct measure of concentration unless other conditions are controlled.

An acid reacting with a base can form salt and water (neutralisation). For example:

`HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O`

Acids may also react with some metals to produce a salt and hydrogen gas, and with carbonates to produce a salt, water, and carbon dioxide. These reactions should be tested only in a supervised laboratory.

Many everyday substances are salts. Sodium chloride is used as a food seasoning and industrial raw material. Baking soda, washing soda, and plaster of Paris have distinct compositions and uses; do not treat these names as interchangeable. Always check the formula and preparation conditions in your prescribed text.

**Misconception check:** “All acids are dangerous and all bases are safe” is false. Hazard depends on the substance and concentration. Never taste chemicals or identify them by touch.


### Practice questions and feedback

**C3.** A student adds universal indicator to two solutions. A turns red and B turns blue. Which is acidic and which is basic? What additional evidence would help compare how strongly acidic they are?

- **C3:** A is acidic; B is basic. Compare the indicator colours with a pH scale or use a suitable calibrated pH method. Red/blue alone does not quantify the pH.

### Safety and practical learning

Possible class investigations include observing a teacher-demonstrated reaction, comparing indicator colours, or modelling metal displacement. These are learning suggestions, not a confirmed official practical list. Use eye protection and teacher-approved quantities; never mix unknown chemicals, heat sealed containers, or inhale gases directly.
