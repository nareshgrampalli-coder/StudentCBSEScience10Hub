## Chapter 13: Our Environment

### Learning goals

Interpret food chains and webs, distinguish matter cycling from energy flow, and evaluate environmental impacts.

An ecosystem includes living organisms and the non-living conditions with which they interact. Producers, such as green plants, use an energy source to make organic matter. Consumers obtain matter by feeding on other organisms. Decomposers break down dead material and wastes, returning nutrients to the environment.

A **food chain** is a simplified representation of feeding relationships. Arrows point from the food to the organism that consumes it, showing the direction of matter and energy transfer. A **food web** connects multiple food chains and better represents many ecosystems. Organisms can occupy different roles depending on what they eat.

Energy flows through an ecosystem and is transferred between trophic levels; it is not recycled in the same way as matter. Some energy becomes unavailable for transfer at each step, including through heat released during life processes. Nutrients such as carbon and water cycle through living organisms and the environment.

### Human impacts and responsible choices

Pollution can affect air, water, and soil. Persistent chemicals may accumulate in organisms and become more concentrated at higher trophic levels. Habitat loss and overuse of resources can disrupt ecological relationships. Waste reduction, reuse, recycling, habitat protection, and informed resource use can reduce some pressures, but the effectiveness of an action depends on local conditions and implementation.

**Worked example.** In the chain `grass → grasshopper → frog → snake`, the arrows show matter and energy moving from food to consumer. If a pesticide persists and accumulates in organisms, predators higher in the chain may receive higher concentrations. This is a possible ecological pattern, not a claim that every chemical behaves identically.

### Common misconceptions

- Food-chain arrows do not point toward the organism being eaten; they point toward the consumer.
- Matter cycles, but energy flows through the ecosystem and ultimately disperses.
- Removing one organism can affect multiple relationships in a food web.
- “Natural” does not automatically mean safe; assess evidence and exposure.

### Practice questions

**R1.** In `algae → small fish → large fish`, what does each arrow represent?

**R2.** Explain why decomposers are important even though they are not always shown in a short food chain.

**R3.** Give one reason a food web can be more informative than a single food chain.

### Answers and feedback

- **R1:** The direction of matter and energy transfer from food to consumer: algae are eaten by small fish; small fish are eaten by large fish.
- **R2:** Decomposers break down dead organisms and wastes, returning nutrients for reuse in ecosystems. Without decomposition, nutrients would remain locked in dead material.
- **R3:** A food web shows interconnected feeding relationships and alternative food sources, so it better represents ecosystem complexity.

### Suggested learning activity

Use a textbook or teacher-approved local example to build a food web. Clearly label each organism and the evidence for each feeding link. Avoid collecting organisms or damaging habitats. This is a suggested activity, not a confirmed official practical requirement.
