# Unit II — World of Living

## Learning goals

Explain how organisms obtain and use materials; connect structure to function in transport, gas exchange, and excretion; describe coordination and reproduction; and reason about inheritance using evidence and simple models.

## 1. Life processes

Living organisms need energy and materials. **Nutrition** is the acquisition and use of food. Green plants use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen. Chlorophyll and suitable light are needed. Animals obtain food by consuming other organisms and digesting it into absorbable molecules.

**Respiration** releases usable energy from food through chemical reactions in cells. It is not the same as breathing: breathing exchanges gases with the environment, while cellular respiration occurs in cells. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and releases more energy per glucose molecule than anaerobic pathways.

### Transport

In humans, blood transports dissolved substances and cells through a closed network. The heart pumps blood; arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins return blood to it, and capillaries allow exchange with tissues. The direction is defined relative to the heart, not by whether the blood is oxygen-rich. Plants move water and minerals mainly through xylem and transport sugars through phloem; transpiration can contribute to the movement of water.

### Excretion

Excretion removes metabolic wastes. Human kidneys filter blood and help regulate water and dissolved substances; urine travels through the urinary system. Plants may store wastes in tissues or release some to their surroundings. Excretion is distinct from egestion, the removal of undigested food from the digestive tract.

**Worked explanation.** During exercise, muscle cells use energy faster. Breathing and heart rates rise to increase oxygen delivery and carbon dioxide removal. This is a coordinated response to increased demand, not evidence that respiration occurs only in the lungs.

## 2. Control and coordination

Organisms detect changes and respond. In animals, receptors detect stimuli; sensory neurons carry signals to the central nervous system; motor neurons carry instructions to effectors such as muscles. A reflex pathway can produce a rapid response, but the brain still receives information about many reflexes.

Hormones are chemical messengers transported to target tissues. Compared with many nerve signals, hormonal responses may begin more slowly and last longer. Plant hormones influence growth and responses. A shoot bending toward light is a directional growth response: cells on opposite sides grow at different rates.

**Misconception check:** The nervous and hormonal systems are not competing explanations. They coordinate different functions and can interact.

## 3. How do organisms reproduce?

Reproduction produces new organisms; it is not required for an individual organism to stay alive. Asexual reproduction typically involves one parent and produces offspring with very similar inherited information, although variation can still arise. Sexual reproduction involves the joining of gametes and produces new combinations of inherited information.

In flowering plants, pollen carries male gametes to the female part of a flower. After fertilisation, the ovule develops into a seed and the ovary may develop into a fruit. Human reproduction involves specialised reproductive organs and gametes. Puberty is a period of physical development; it does not by itself imply emotional or social readiness for parenthood.

Reproductive health includes reliable information, personal safety, consent, hygiene, and access to appropriate healthcare. For personal medical decisions, consult a qualified health professional rather than relying on a study guide.

**L4.** Give one biological advantage and one limitation of asexual reproduction compared with sexual reproduction.

## 4. Heredity

Heredity is the transfer of biological information from parents to offspring. Genes are sections of DNA; alternative forms of a gene are called alleles. In a simple inherited trait model, an organism receives one allele from each parent. A dominant allele can affect the observable trait when only one copy is present; a recessive allele is expressed in the simplified model when two recessive copies are inherited. Real traits are often influenced by several genes and the environment.

**Worked example (model, not a prediction about people).** If `T` represents a dominant allele and `t` a recessive allele, a `Tt × Tt` cross has possible combinations `TT`, `Tt`, `Tt`, and `tt`. In this model, three combinations show the dominant phenotype and one the recessive phenotype. This is a probability across many offspring, not a guarantee for a small family.

Inherited variations can affect survival and reproduction in a particular environment. Over many generations, populations may change. Acquired changes to an individual's body do not automatically alter the inherited DNA in their reproductive cells.

### Feedback and answers

- **L1:** Breathing is air movement/gas exchange with the environment; cellular respiration is energy-releasing chemistry in cells. Excretion removes metabolic wastes; egestion removes undigested food.
- **L2:** Arteries carry blood away from the heart. Blood oxygen level depends on the vessel and circulation pathway; the pulmonary artery is an important counterexample.
- **L3:** Receptors detect heat/pain; sensory neurons carry signals to the spinal cord/central nervous system; a motor pathway activates muscles to withdraw. A rapid response can reduce tissue damage.
- **L4:** Asexual reproduction can be rapid and needs no partner; limited genetic variation can reduce adaptability when conditions change. Sexual reproduction can create combinations of variation but uses more time/energy and requires gametes to meet.
- **L5:** One of four model outcomes, or 25%. Each conception is a new random event under the model, so four offspring need not show exactly one recessive phenotype.
- **L6:** Training changes muscle development, not necessarily the DNA sequence in reproductive cells. Inheritance concerns information passed through reproductive cells.

Use diagrams, models, and teacher-directed observation for human reproductive biology. Respect privacy and use accurate, age-appropriate language. Do not infer a person's health or traits from a simplified inheritance exercise.
