## Unit IV — Effects of Current: Electricity and Magnetism

## Learning goals

Relate current, potential difference, and resistance; solve simple circuit problems; explain electrical power and heating; and use field models to describe magnets, electromagnets, and the magnetic effect of current.

## 1. Electricity

Electric current is the rate of flow of charge: `I = Q/t`. Its SI unit is the ampere (A). Potential difference is energy transferred per unit charge: `V = W/Q`, measured in volts (V). Resistance describes opposition to current and is measured in ohms (Ω).

For a conductor under conditions where its physical state remains constant, Ohm's law states `V = IR`. A straight-line voltage-current graph through the origin is consistent with this relation. If temperature or another condition changes, resistance may also change.

### Resistors in circuits

In a series circuit, the same current passes through each component and the total resistance is the sum: `Rtotal = R1 + R2 + ...`. In a parallel circuit, the potential difference across each branch is the same; for ideal resistors, `1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...`. Adding a parallel path reduces the equivalent resistance.

**Worked example.** A `6 Ω` resistor is connected across `12 V`. Using `I = V/R`, the current is `12/6 = 2 A`. The units check: `V/Ω = A`.

### Heating and power

Electrical energy can be transferred as heat. For a resistor, the heating relation is `H = I²Rt` when current and resistance are constant. Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer: `P = VI`; combining with Ohm's law gives `P = I²R` and `P = V²/R`. Household energy use is often billed in kilowatt-hours (kWh); `1 kWh` is the energy used by a `1 kW` device operating for `1 hour`, not a unit of power.

**E1.** A `4 Ω` resistor has a `2 A` current. Find its potential difference and power.

**E2.** Two resistors, `3 Ω` and `6 Ω`, are in parallel. Find equivalent resistance and explain why it is less than either resistor.

## 2. Magnetic effects of electric current

A magnetic field describes the region where a magnetic force can be detected. Field lines are a model: outside a bar magnet they are drawn from north to south, and closer lines indicate a stronger field. Field lines do not cross because the field at one point cannot point in two directions at once.

An electric current produces a magnetic field around its conductor. The field around a straight wire forms circles; its direction depends on current direction. A coil strengthens the field, and a coil with a magnetic core can act as an electromagnet. Increasing current or the number of turns generally strengthens the electromagnet, within practical limits.

A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field can experience a force. The direction depends on the field and current directions. An electric motor uses this effect to produce motion. A changing magnetic field can induce current in a conductor; a generator uses electromagnetic induction to convert mechanical energy into electrical energy. Do not confuse the motor effect (current and field produce force) with induction (changing magnetic conditions produce an emf/current).

### Common misconceptions

- Current is not “used up” as it passes through a series circuit; energy is transferred to components.
- A voltmeter is connected in parallel with the component; an ammeter is connected in series in the ideal school circuit.
- A magnetic field line is a representation, not a physical string.
- More voltage does not always mean more current if resistance or circuit conditions also change.

### Check understanding

**E3.** A `10 Ω` resistor is connected to `5 V`. Calculate current and power.

**E4.** State the difference between the operating principle of a simple motor and that of a generator.

### Feedback and answers

- **E1:** `V = IR = 2 × 4 = 8 V`. `P = VI = 8 × 2 = 16 W`.
- **E2:** `1/R = 1/3 + 1/6 = 1/2`, so `R = 2 Ω`. Parallel paths allow more total charge flow for the same potential difference; equivalent resistance is below either branch resistance.
- **E3:** `I = V/R = 5/10 = 0.5 A`; `P = VI = 5 × 0.5 = 2.5 W`.
- **E4:** A motor uses the force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field to produce motion. A generator uses electromagnetic induction from changing magnetic conditions to produce an emf/current.

### Retrieval check

Draw a series circuit containing a cell, switch, resistor, ammeter, and voltmeter. Show the correct meter connections. Then explain in words how total resistance changes when an additional resistor is connected in parallel.

## Laboratory safety

Never connect an ammeter directly across a cell or mains supply. Use low-voltage school equipment only under teacher supervision. Switch off circuits before changing connections; resistors and wires can become hot. Never handle household mains wiring as a classroom activity.
