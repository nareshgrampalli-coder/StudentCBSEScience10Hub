## Unit I — Chemical Substances: Nature and Behaviour

## Learning goals

By the end of this unit, you should be able to represent chemical change with balanced equations; relate observable properties to particles and bonding; explain the behaviour of acids, bases, metals, and carbon compounds; and use evidence to support a chemical conclusion.

## 1. Chemical reactions and equations

### Key vocabulary

**Reactant:** starting substance in a reaction. **Product:** substance formed. **Precipitate:** insoluble solid produced in a solution. **Oxidation:** addition of oxygen or removal of hydrogen in the school-level model. **Reduction:** removal of oxygen or addition of hydrogen. **Corrosion:** gradual damage to a material by its surroundings.

Atoms are rearranged in a chemical reaction; they are not created or destroyed. A chemical equation represents this rearrangement. The number of atoms of each element must therefore be the same on both sides. Change coefficients to balance an equation; do not alter the small numbers within a chemical formula.

**Worked example.** Balance hydrogen reacting with oxygen to form water.

Start with `H2 + O2 → H2O`. Two water molecules are needed to match the two oxygen atoms: `H2 + O2 → 2H2O`. There are now four hydrogen atoms on the right, so use two hydrogen molecules on the left:

`2H2 + O2 → 2H2O`

Check: four H atoms and two O atoms on each side.

### Common reaction patterns

- **Combination:** two or more reactants form one product; for example, `2Mg + O2 → 2MgO`.
- **Decomposition:** one compound breaks into simpler substances, often with heat, light, or electricity supplied.
- **Displacement:** a more reactive element replaces a less reactive element in a compound.
- **Double displacement:** ions exchange partners; a precipitate, gas, or water may form.
- **Oxidation and reduction:** these can occur together in the same reaction. For instance, in `CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O`, copper(II) oxide loses oxygen (reduction) while hydrogen gains oxygen (oxidation).

State symbols such as `(s)`, `(l)`, `(g)`, and `(aq)` identify physical state or solution. Add them only when the states are known.

### Check understanding

**C1.** Balance `Al + O2 → Al2O3`.

**C2.** In `Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu`, identify the reaction pattern and explain what the observation of a copper coating would indicate.

## 2. Acids, bases, and salts

Acids in aqueous solution produce hydrogen ions; bases in aqueous solution produce hydroxide ions in the introductory model. An indicator changes colour over a range of acidity. Litmus distinguishes acidic and basic conditions, while universal indicator gives an approximate pH colour. A lower pH indicates a more acidic solution and a higher pH a more basic solution; pH is not a direct measure of concentration unless other conditions are controlled.

An acid reacting with a base can form salt and water (neutralisation). For example:

`HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O`

Acids may also react with some metals to produce a salt and hydrogen gas, and with carbonates to produce a salt, water, and carbon dioxide. These reactions should be tested only in a supervised laboratory.

Many everyday substances are salts. Sodium chloride is used as a food seasoning and industrial raw material. Baking soda, washing soda, and plaster of Paris have distinct compositions and uses; do not treat these names as interchangeable. Always check the formula and preparation conditions in your prescribed text.

**Misconception check:** “All acids are dangerous and all bases are safe” is false. Hazard depends on the substance and concentration. Never taste chemicals or identify them by touch.

**C3.** A student adds universal indicator to two solutions. A turns red and B turns blue. Which is acidic and which is basic? What additional evidence would help compare how strongly acidic they are?

## 3. Metals and non-metals

Metals are generally lustrous, malleable, ductile, and good conductors, while non-metals often have contrasting properties. These are patterns, not definitions without exceptions. Physical properties alone do not tell you how a substance will react.

Metals differ in reactivity. A more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from its salt solution. This provides evidence for an experimentally established reactivity series. Metals react with oxygen to form oxides; some metal oxides are basic, while some are amphoteric (they react with both acids and bases). Non-metal oxides are often acidic.

Some metals occur as compounds in ores. Extraction depends on reactivity and the nature of the ore; it may involve concentration, chemical conversion, or reduction. Do not assume one extraction method applies to every metal. Corrosion can be slowed by barriers such as paint or by other protective methods; prevention works by limiting the material's contact with the damaging environment.

**Worked example.** Iron placed in copper sulphate solution can acquire a copper coating because iron is more reactive than copper and displaces it:

`Fe + CuSO4 → FeSO4 + Cu`

This conclusion follows from the observed displacement, not from colour alone.

**C4.** A clean metal strip is placed in a solution of a salt of another metal. No visible change occurs. Can you conclude that the strip is less reactive? State one limitation of this test.

## 4. Carbon and its compounds

Carbon forms many compounds because a carbon atom can form four covalent bonds and carbon atoms can link to one another. A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons. Simple molecular compounds do not generally conduct electricity because they lack freely moving charged particles.

Organic compounds can form a **homologous series**: members share a functional group and general pattern, and successive members differ by a `CH2` unit. A functional group strongly influences chemical behaviour. Learn the series and functional groups prescribed by the current syllabus rather than extending the list from memory alone.

Combustion of a carbon compound in sufficient oxygen can produce carbon dioxide and water; limited oxygen can lead to incomplete combustion and carbon monoxide or soot. Ethanol and ethanoic acid have different functional groups and properties. Ethanoic acid reacts with a carbonate to release carbon dioxide. Soap molecules have a water-attracting end and an oil-attracting end, helping disperse grease in water. Detergents can clean in water conditions where soap forms scum more readily.

### Check understanding

**C5.** Explain why changing a coefficient can balance an equation but changing a formula's subscript changes the substance.

**C6.** A hydrocarbon burns with a smoky flame. Give a likely explanation and one way to make combustion more complete.

### Feedback and answers

- **C1:** `4Al + 3O2 → 2Al2O3`. Both sides have four Al and six O atoms.
- **C2:** Displacement. A copper coating is consistent with copper ions being reduced to copper metal as zinc is oxidised to zinc ions. Other evidence, such as a change in solution colour, may support the inference.
- **C3:** A is acidic; B is basic. Compare the indicator colours with a pH scale or use a suitable calibrated pH method. Red/blue alone does not quantify the pH.
- **C4:** No. A metal that is less reactive may fail to displace the other metal, but a failed test can also result from an oxide coating, unsuitable conditions, or an observation error. Repeat with controls and a known reactive metal.
- **C5:** A coefficient changes the number of molecules represented, preserving each formula. A subscript changes the atom ratio and therefore the identity of the compound.
- **C6:** Limited oxygen can cause incomplete combustion and soot. Adequate air/oxygen and an appropriate burner setting promote more complete combustion; never adjust a flame without supervision.

### Retrieval check

Without looking back, describe how you would verify a balanced equation, name two observations that can indicate chemical change, and explain why an observation alone may not prove a reaction mechanism.

## Safety and practical learning

Possible class investigations include observing a teacher-demonstrated reaction, comparing indicator colours, or modelling metal displacement. These are learning suggestions, not a confirmed official practical list. Use eye protection and teacher-approved quantities; never mix unknown chemicals, heat sealed containers, or inhale gases directly.
