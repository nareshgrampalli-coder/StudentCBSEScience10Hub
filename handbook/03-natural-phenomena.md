# Unit III — Natural Phenomena: Light and the Human Eye

## Learning goals

Use ray models to explain reflection and refraction; calculate image positions with the sign convention taught in class; relate lenses to vision; and describe dispersion using evidence.

## 1. Reflection and refraction

Light travels in straight lines through a uniform transparent medium in the ray model. At a reflecting surface, the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection; both angles are measured from the normal, an imaginary line perpendicular to the surface. Curved mirrors can form images whose nature and size depend on object position.

When light crosses a boundary between transparent media, its speed changes and its path may bend. The ray bends toward the normal when it enters a medium in which it travels more slowly, and away from the normal when it enters a medium in which it travels faster. Refraction explains why a partially submerged straight object can appear bent.

For spherical mirrors and thin lenses, a ray diagram helps locate an image. Principal rays include rays parallel to the principal axis, rays through or directed toward the focus, and rays through the centre of curvature (mirror) or optical centre (thin lens approximation). Use the exact construction and sign convention required by your teacher/text.

Under the common Cartesian sign convention used in school optics, distances are measured from the pole or optical centre, and the direction of incident light is assigned a sign. Do not memorise a formula without its convention. The mirror relation is commonly written `1/f = 1/v + 1/u`; the thin-lens relation is commonly written `1/f = 1/v - 1/u`. Here `u` is object distance, `v` image distance, and `f` focal length. Magnification relations are `m = -v/u` for mirrors and `m = v/u` for lenses under this convention. Verify signs and units before substituting.

**Worked example.** A thin lens has focal length `+20 cm`. Under the stated convention it is converging. An object at `u = -30 cm` forms an image according to `1/20 = 1/v - 1/(-30)`. Thus `1/v = 1/20 - 1/30 = 1/60`, so `v = +60 cm`. The positive image distance indicates a real image on the side opposite the object under this convention. Check the ray diagram to confirm the interpretation.

## 2. The human eye and dispersion

The eye's lens focuses light on the retina. The iris changes pupil size, helping regulate the amount of light entering the eye. Accommodation changes lens shape to focus near and distant objects. In myopia, distant objects are not focused on the retina; a diverging lens is used for correction. In hypermetropia, near objects are not focused on the retina; a converging lens is used. Astigmatism is associated with unequal focusing in different directions and requires an appropriate prescription.

White light contains a range of visible wavelengths. A prism can refract these wavelengths by different amounts, spreading the light into a spectrum (dispersion). A rainbow involves refraction, internal reflection, and dispersion in water droplets.

**Common errors**

- Measuring angles from the surface rather than the normal.
- Treating every image as real; a virtual image cannot be projected onto a screen.
- Using a lens or mirror formula with distances in mixed units.
- Naming an eye defect without describing where the image tends to form.

### Check understanding

**N1.** A ray makes an angle of incidence of `35°` with the normal. What is its angle of reflection?

**N2.** A student uses a converging spectacle lens for a person whose near vision is blurred. Which common defect is being corrected? State the general lens type used for the other common defect, myopia.

**N3.** Why does a prism spread white light into colours?

### Feedback and answers

- **N1:** `35°`, measured from the normal.
- **N2:** Hypermetropia is commonly corrected with a converging lens. Myopia is commonly corrected with a diverging lens.
- **N3:** Different wavelengths are refracted by different amounts in the prism, so they emerge in different directions.

### Retrieval check

Sketch a labelled ray diagram for a converging lens with an object beyond twice the focal length. Mark the principal axis, optical centre, focus, object, image, and at least two principal rays. State whether the image is real or virtual and how its size compares with the object.

## Safe practical learning

Ray boxes, mirrors, lenses, and prisms can be used for teacher-supervised investigations. Never direct intense light or a laser into anyone's eyes. Record apparatus, distances, units, and observations; distinguish observed results from the ray-model explanation.
